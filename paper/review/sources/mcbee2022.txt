Abstract
Advancements in deep learning techniques have proved useful in biomedical image segmentation. However, the large amount of unlabeled data inherent in biomedical imagery, particularly in digital pathology, creates a semi-supervised learning paradigm. Specifically, because of the time consuming nature of producing pixel-wise annotations and the high cost of having a pathologist dedicate time to labeling, there is a large amount of unlabeled data that we wish to utilize in training segmentation algorithms. Pseudo-labeling is one method to leverage the unlabeled data to increase overall model performance. We adapt a method used for image classification pseudo-labeling to select images for segmentation pseudo-labeling and apply it to 3 digital pathology datasets. To select images for pseudo-labeling, we create and explore different thresholds for confidence and uncertainty on an image level basis. Furthermore, we study the relationship between image-level uncertainty and confidence with model performance. We find that the certainty metrics do not consistently correlate with performance intuitively, and abnormal correlations serve as an indicator of a model’s ability to produce pseudo-labels that are useful in training.
I. INTRODUCTION
Deep learning advancements have improved biomedical image analysis, resulting in automated disease screening [
1
] and quantification [
2
]. The U-Net [
3
] improved cell and nuclear segmentation in microscopy imagery. The U-Net has also been applied to digital histopathology [
2
], where patches are extracted from large whole-slide-images (WSIs) and receive pixel-wise annotations. Each WSI is on the order of multiple gigabytes of data, and the segmentation annotations are inherently cumbersome. Thus, a pathologist is only able to annotate a small portion of a whole slide image per patient, leaving most of the WSIs unlabeled and unused. This context invites the use of semi-supervised learning (SSL), which seeks to leverage both labeled and unlabeled data to increase a model’s performance on a hold out test set. One tool to utilize this unlabeled data is pseudo-labeling.
First introduced by Lee [
4
], pseudo-labeling is assigning an unlabeled observation the classification label using the maximum prediction probability of the model trained on labeled data. A model generates pseudo-labels for a number of inputs, and then the model trains on the pseudo-labels as well as the labeled data. Pseudo-labels have also been used for consistency regularization on unlabeled imagery in the image classification context [
5
]. There, the model is trained by minimizing the entropy between the pseudo-labels of a weakly augmented image and the output predictions of a strongly augmented image. In this method, only images with pseudo-labels with a confidence above a given threshold are used. Confidence is the magnitude of the model’s maximum output class probability. Using a minimum confidence threshold to select which pseudo-labels to use is a means of filtering out pseudo-labels which would not be helpful to the model. Rizve et al. [
6
] develops a more sophisticated method of selecting pseudo-labels for image classification by setting a maximum uncertainty threshold as well as minimum confidence threshold. Only the pseudo-labels for which the model has high confidence and low uncertainty are selected for training, since these pseudo-labels are likely to be more accurate than those with low confidence and high uncertainty. In contrast to the image classification context in [
6
], a segmentation model produces confidence and uncertainty values for each pixel. Pixel-wise uncertainty has been used in training segmentation models by weighting each pixel by its uncertainty [
7
]. However, to our knowledge, current literature lacks an analysis of image-level confidence and uncertainty values to select images for pseudo-labeling for segmentation. Such an approach would simplify the computational cost of pixel-wise weighting.
Our contributions are the following. 1) We adapt the confidence and image thresholding technique in [
6
] to create image-level confidence and uncertainty measures to select images for pseudo-labeling in binary segmentation. 2) We create adaptive functions for selection and weighting of pseudo-labels for training. 3) We show the ability of the relationship between image-level uncertainty and performance on labeled data in indicating overall model performance on a hold out test set on 3 separate digital histopathology datasets.
II. Datasets
We use three histopathology image datasets in this study: multi-organ nuclei segmentation (MoNuSeg) [
8
], Colorectal Nuclear Segmentation and the Pheno-types (CoNSeP) [
9
], and eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) [
2
]. Each dataset is derived from tissue biopsy and contains patches extracted from whole-slide images (WSIs) stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE), scanned at 40× objective magnification. All datasets are approached as binary segmentation problems, with nuclei annotated in MoNuSeg and CoNSeP and eosinophils annotated in EoE.
MoNuSeg:
MoNuSeg consists of 44 biopsies from tumors of various organs of multiple patients, where each biopsy is presented as a 1000 by 1000 pixel image with pixel-wise nuclei annotations. Each image is sliced into 500 × 500 pixel sub-images with no overlap, resulting in 176 images.
CoNSeP:
CoNSeP consists of images from patients diagnosed with colorectal adenocarcinoma (CRA). The nuclei for 6 different cell types are annotated: normal epithelial; tumor epithelial; inflammatory; necrotic cell debris; muscle and fibroblasts. To simplify the problem, all nuclei are collapsed into a single type. Each of the 41 image is sliced into 500 × 500 pixel sub-images with no overlap, resulting in 164 images.
EoE:
The EoE dataset consists of images from 30 patients diagnosed with eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE). EoE is a progressive disease that presents as episodes of vomiting, dysphagia, and heartburn. Chronic inflammation and tissue remodeling can lead to luminal narrowing due to the formation of strictures and extensive fibrosis. The gold standard for diagnosis is tissue biopsy with greater than or equal to 15 intraepithelial eosinophils per high power field on light microscopy [
10
], [
11
]. Eosinophils are proinflammatory cells with biolobed nuclei which appear as two nuclei pressed into one another to make a figure 8 pattern. The cytoplasm is filled with red-staining secretory granules on HE staining [
12
]. Tissue eosinophils can be seen on light microscopy in various states of degranulation. Intact eosinophils are annotated in this dataset. Each of the 514 images has a size of 512 × 512 pixels.

A. Examine Correlations
We begin with examining the correlations between image-level model performance on the labeled dataset, referenced in

For entropy, we average the output probabilities of the MC dropout models and then calculate the entropy per pixel. Since each pixel is assigned uncertainty and confidence values, the values are averaged across all pixels per image to achieve a single image-level value, and they are plotted against the model performance per image. The central assumption in using confidence and uncertainty in pseudo-label selection is that as confidence increases and uncertainty decreases, the quality of the pseudo-labels should also increase. Investigating these correlations allows us to assess the robustness of this assumption for image-level confidence and uncertainty values for the segmentation task. Moreover, a model which violates this assumption would not be able to differentiate between pseudo-labels of varying quality. Also, we are not able to examine these correlations for the unlabeled dataset, because, by definition, we do not have the true labels for them to calculate the dice coefficient. Thus, the magnitude and direction of these correlation for the labeled dataset is the best proxy for how the model would behave on the unlabeled data, and so indicate the model’s usefulness in pseudo-label image selection.

B. Pseudo-Label Selection via Thresholds and Weighting
In line with [
6
], we set minimum confidence and maximum uncertainty thresholds to select which unlabeled images to pseudo-label and use for training for each dataset. C. Model Setup and Training
A U-Net architecture was used with randomly initialized weights for each seed per dataset. The Adam optimizer and a learning rate of 2e-4 were used. Each model was trained on half of the training data and the other half was used as the unlabeled set for pseudo-labeling. For the initial labeled training, we used early stopping of 12 epochs without improvement in the validation loss, up to 100 epochs total. After this, uncertainty and confidence values were quantified for each unlabeled image. Then, the relevant images were selected for pseudo-labeling and assigned weights for the loss function for each technique mentioned. The model was trained for 3 epochs using labeled and pseudo-labeled data, where the model with the best validation accuracy was saved. No pseudo-labels were included in the validation set. The process repeated until the model performance on the validation set did not increase for 3 rounds of training on pseudo-labels. The converged model was then evaluated against a hold out test set.
IV. Experiments and Results
We first examine the correlations of the image-level confidence and uncertainty values with the model performance per image. This is realized as the dice coefficient of the pseudo-labels and the true labels using 8 randomly initialized models on the labeled dataset. Each model’s dice coefficient against a test set is also evaluated. Additionally, the expected calibration error (ECE) [
15
] is used to determine whether model calibration corresponds to changes in the correlations or performance. Each model for a given dataset has the same validation set, but the labeled and unlabeled datasets are randomly partitioned for each seed and are equal in size.
A. Correlation Results
For the EoE dataset in
Table I
, we see that the image-level uncertainty values for images in seeds 4 and 6 have a positive or zero correlation with the image-level training dice coefficients. Also, the image-level confidence values for these seeds have a negative correlation with dice coefficients. This signifies that as the uncertainty for a given image decreases and its confidence increases, the model’s performance on that image decreases. This is counter-intuitive, since the assumption of pseudo-labeling is that as uncertainty decreases and confidence increases, the pseudo-labels’ usefulness in an image, measured here by the dice coefficient between the pseudo-labels and the true labels, should increase. This correlation, though in the minority of the randomly initialized runs, does demonstrate an instance where the image-level confidence and uncertainty values from a model are not useful in selecting pseudo-labels, since minimizing image-level uncertainty results in selecting images with less accurate pseudo-labels. Furthermore, the models with these unexpected correlations do not present a consistently poor test dice coefficient or low ECE compared to the models which have the expected correlations. Thus, neither poor initialization nor calibration accounts for this correlation phenomenon.
For the CoNSeP dataset in
Table I
, the image-level confidence and entropy values for images in seeds 2, 3, 4, 5, and 7 have negative and positive correlations, respectively, with the image-level training dice values. Again, these correlations undermine the central assumption in selecting pseudo-labels. However, these seeds have a noticeably lower test dice coefficient than those with the expected correlations, seeds 0, 1, and 6. Welch’s t-test confirms with a p-value of 0.007 that the average test dice coefficient of the seeds with the expected correlations are higher than those without it. The ECE values do not account for the correlation or performance differences.
For the MoNuSeg dataset in
Table I
, the image-level entropy values for seeds 0 and 2 have a positive correlation with the image-level training dice values. Again, this indicates that as uncertainty increases the accuracy of the pseudo-labels increases as well. The image-level confidence values for seed 0 correlate negatively with image-level training performance. The test performance for seed 0 is low, which is statistically significant according to a t-test comparing it to the other models’ test dice scores. The test dice score is not abnormal for seed 2, possibly due to its positive confidence correlation with the dice score. The model for seed 2 has the lowest ECE value, and the ECE values for other seeds are closely grouped, thus poor model calibration does not account for these correlation or performance differences.

V. Discussion
Across the three datasets, randomly initialized models did not always have the expected correlations of image-level confidence and uncertainty with image-level performance. This contradicts an intuitive assumption of pseudo-labels: as model certainty increases, the quality of the pseudo-labels should increase as well. It is possible that the differences in these metrics are averaged out when the entire image is taken into account. Furthermore, models for the EoE dataset increased performance when trained on pseudo-labels, regardless of the individual model’s correlations. This may be due in part to the sparsity of the EoE dataset. Since very few Eos are contained in each image, the model may be too conservative due to class imbalance. Pseudo-labels, even if not always correct, may help with addressing the bias in the data of a lack of Eos.
Though not robust, the correlations did prove informative in certain contexts. For MoNuSeg and CoNSeP, the models where the confidence correlated negatively and the entropy correlated positively with the training dice performed worse than those models with the opposite correlations in a manner that was statistically significant. This indicates that if a model has certainty metrics that do not correlate with the performance as expected, it may have worse performance on the test set than a model that has the expected correlations.
For CoNSeP, using the minimum 25% entropy pseudo-labels worsened the model performance for the poorly trained seeds, whereas for the models with the expected correlations were not negatively impacted in a significant way by any of the pseudo-label weightings.
VI. Conclusion
Before using pseudo-labeling, examining the correlations between certainty metrics and a model’s performance on labeled imagery may help indicate whether a model’s uncertainty about an image is informative. Thus, a positive correlation for confidence and negative correlation for uncertainty are necessary but not sufficient indicators of the utility of pseudo-labeling for a given model. Furthermore, averaging certainty metrics across an image may obfuscate the complexity inherent in segmentation, particularly when there is a class imbalance. Further work examining these correlations on a pixel-wise basis would be insightful.
Clinical relevance —
The proposed approach adapts image-level confidence and uncertainty measures for segmentation pseudo-labeling on digital pathology datasets. Increased model performance enables better disease quantification for histopathology.
