Artifact Augmentation for Learning-based Quality Control of Whole
Slide Images
Artur Jurgas1,2, Marek Wodzinski1,2, Weronika Celniak1,2, Manfredo Atzori1,3 and Henning M¨uller1,4
Abstract— The acquisition of whole slide images is prone to
artifacts that can require human control and re-scanning, both
in clinical workflows and in research-oriented settings. Quality
control algorithms are a first step to overcome this challenge,
as they limit the use of low quality images. Developing quality
control systems in histopathology is not straightforward, also
due to the limited availability of data related to this topic.
We address the problem by proposing a tool to augment data
with artifacts. The proposed method seamlessly generates
and blends artifacts from an external library to a given
histopathology
dataset.
The
datasets
augmented
by
the
blended artifacts are then used to train an artifact detection
network in a supervised way. We use the YOLOv5 model for
the artifact detection with a slightly modified training pipeline.
The proposed tool can be extended into a complete framework
for the quality assessment of whole slide images.
Clinical relevance— The proposed method may be useful
for the initial quality screening of whole slide images. Each
year, millions of whole slide images are acquired and digitized
worldwide. Numerous of them contain artifacts affecting the
following AI-oriented analysis. Therefore, a tool operating at the
acquisition phase and improving the initial quality assessment
is crucial to increase the performance of digital pathology
algorithms, e.g., early cancer diagnosis.
I. INTRODUCTION
Quality control (QC) of histopathology whole slide images
(WSIs) is crucial for further processing and analysis of the
images. The WSI acquisition is a complex process and prone
to artifacts. Artifacts are a side effect of many stages of
the sample preparation like fixation, microtomy, staining, or
mounting procedures [1]. They also include scratches of the
coverslip, tissue tearing, residual wax impacting staining, and
others.
The artifacts impact a wide range of WSI processing and
analysis techniques. They influence the field of view and can
hide crucial tissue structures for diagnosis. QC systems can
help mitigate this by catching low quality images just after
their acquisition and improve the overall performance.

semi-automatic HistoQC software that allows for automatic

A number of authors also present learning-based QC
systems, like [4], [5]. While providing good results, they are
limited to blur and out-of-focus artifact types, which were

The authors of [6] proposed a supervised, learning-based
method. They used a private, labeled dataset for training.
Although the approach is successful, it is costly and with
limited generalizability since it requires numerous manual
annotations. Similar work by [7] uses a deep residual net-
work that also uses a limited, manually annotated dataset.
The process of manually annotating WSI artifacts is costly
and time-consuming. It is often hard to find the resources and
time to spend on artifact annotation, as it is often preferred
to use the resources to annotate the WSIs with respect to the
diagnosis.
There are a few that, instead of using an annotated
dataset, synthetically generate artifacts. [8] uses fully syn-
thetic artifacts to test the degradation of previously trained
classification and segmentation models. Our work differs
from it by using a real, nonsynthesized artifacts. Additionally,
the previous work did not propose a method to detect those
artifacts.
The authors in [9] stress the importance of QC in learning-
based histopathology methods. They use mostly synthetic ar-
tifacts, with a limited number of nonsynthetic ones. Similarly
to [8] their quality control applies to the trained models rather
than images.
Contribution:
Our contribution is two-fold. First, we
propose a framework to cut out and modify artifacts with
known ground-truth and use them to augment real-world
histopathology datasets. Second, we modify a common cost
function to allow a semi-supervised training process with
datasets generated by our method. This work can also be
used to further expand model stress testing proposed by [8]
and [9]. Unlike other methods, we do not generate purely
synthetic artifacts but rather propose a method to seamlessly
augment the existing ones and blend them into various
datasets to improve the generalizability of quality assessment
models. Moreover, we treat the task as an object detection
problem at a given resolution level rather than a patch-based
classification, which makes it faster during inference and
more useful in clinical practice.

II. METHODS
A. Overview
The proposed method consists of the following steps: (i)
artifact acquisition: labeling data from the donor dataset, (ii)
artifact cloning: blending acquired artifacts with the destina-
tion dataset with morphological augmentations, (iii) model
training: using the YOLOv5 object detection model with
modified loss function to fine-tune it using the augmented
dataset. The overview of the pipeline is shown in Figure 1.
B. Artifact types and acquisition
The process starts with annotating a few artifacts in
a source dataset. The extracted artifacts are stored in a
dedicated library with adjustable metadata. The metadata
consists of parameters like artifact size, spatial orientation,
or reasonable limits for geometrical transformations. We
acquire the following types of artifacts: (i) markers, (ii)
scratches, (iii) smudges, (iv) staining anomalies, (v) corner
markings, (vi) foreign bodies, (vii) border glass horizontal,
(viii) border glass vertical, (ix) low blur, (x) high blur. Note
that during the rest of the paper we use only real, augmented
artifacts acquired in this step and not synthetic ones, except
for the blur.
C. Artifact processing and cloning
The labeled artifacts are algorithmically inserted into the
target dataset. The process is repeated several times, and
each time the artifacts are inserted at different locations after
random geometrical transformations using affine warping.
The blending of the artifacts is customized by their position
(e.g., inside, outside, or at the edges of a tissue) and the size
with respect to the image resolution.
Each artifact type requires a different approach to blend
it into the target image, i.e. glass scratches are blended
differently than marker or ink staining. Additionally, some
artifacts such as staining can only be transferred inside their
respective class, i.e. staining artifacts from H&E images
should only be applied to other H&E stained images. Arti-
facts other than staining work independently of the staining
dye.
For markers, corner markings, border glasses and foreign
bodies, we use cloning with bilateral filtering adjusted for
each type. For scratches and smudges, we apply seamless
cloning. For staining artifacts, Reinhard color normalization
is used. Both low and high blur is done by a Gaussian kernel
of different intensities and sizes. Blurring is the only case for
which we generate a fully synthetic artifact.
As a final step for each method, we also apply a gradual,
linear transition between the artifact to the image so that the
pixels on the edges will be a combination of the artifact’s
pixels and the original image.

E. Dataset and experimental setup
We evaluated the proposed approach using the ACROBAT
and ANHIR datasets [11], [12], preprocessed and curated by
the dataset owners.
The ACROBAT dataset consists of WSIs captured by
Hamamatsu Nanozoomer XR at 0.23 µm per pixel (40X).
Each slide has its distinctive staining, either Hematoxilin
and Eozine (H&E) or immunohistochemistry (IHC) with ER,
PGR, HER2, and KI67 dyes. The images are 44404 ± 10525
by 24975 ± 3147. The ACROBAT dataset is used mainly as
a training set.
The ANHIR dataset is more diverse with images from
multiple scanners, tissue types, dyes, and magnifications. The
images are 9430 ± 2905 by 11206 ± 3203. The ANHIR
dataset is more heterogeneous and is used as a testing set to
show the generalizability of the method.
All applied artifacts come from 100 H&E images and
100 IHC images from the ACROBAT dataset. We divide
them into two artifact libraries, namely train and validation
libraries. The summary of the extracted artifacts is presented
in Table I. The artifact libraries were used to augment the
ACROBAT and ANHIR datasets.
This approach resulted in the following datasets: (A1) AC-
ROBAT images augmented with artifacts from the training
library, (A2) ACROBAT images augmented with the artifacts
from the validation library, (B1) ANHIR images augmented
with artifacts from the training library, (B2) ANHIR images
augmented with artifacts from the validation library.
A1 is the training set, A2 is the validation set with respect
to the artifacts, B1 is the validation set with respect to the
images, B2 is the test set since neither images nor artifacts
from this dataset were used during training. The A1 dataset
consists of 2000 samples, A2 has 400, B1 1800, and B2 900.
Additionally, as in an ablation study, we trained the model
on the original, non-augmented data that were used for

III. RESULTS
We evaluate the proposed method using the accuracy of the
individual artifact detection, the AP:0.5 (Average Precision
with intersection over union of 0.5), and recall. Accuracy
in this context means the percentage of true positives of a
given class. We count detections as true positives when the
intersection over union (IoU) between their bounding boxes
and the ground-truth is above a threshold of 0.45 and their
labels match [13].
Recall is the preferred indicator of model performance in
our case. It does not take into account false positives and true
negatives, for which we cannot fully reason about. We still
include the AP indicator as it is the standard for reporting
the performance of detection models.
We show the detection accuracy in Table I, the AP
and Recall in Table II, and the recall-confidence curves in
Figure 2. Accuracy in Table I is presented for the test dataset
B2.
The confidence in Figure 2 in this context means the
threshold at which we make the classification decision with
respect to the model output. This means that even if we
take into consideration weak predictions, artifacts such as
foreign bodies are poorly detected. Similarly, even if we only
consider strong predictions, corner markings, border glasses,
markers, and staining artifacts are still properly detected
which means good model fitness.

IV. DISCUSSION
The results confirm that the proposed concept can result
in a QC system being able to generalize into real-world
histology data. The model generalizes well for the validation
and test sets, even though the training data are limited. The
notable exceptions are: low blur and foreign objects.
The performance for scratches and markers also decreases,
albeit less. The seamless cloning of those artifacts highly
depends on blending the sharp edges of those structures.
This step still experiences high deviations in quality between
different resolutions.

Comparison with other approaches is limited as authors
like [8], [9] do not try to detect the artifacts but rather test
their influence on model performance. The authors of [4],
[5] present a QC system that is limited only to blur and out-
of-focus regions. We achieve lower accuracy than them on
this particular type of artifact; however, it cannot be directly
compared because our method blends the artifacts also in
the background. We achieve similar performance in other
types of artifacts like border glasses and corner markings.
The most similar work to our approach is [7]. The authors,
however, do not specify what data they are using. They
use only three slides for validation and one for testing.
We use a completely different dataset including different
scanners, staining methods and tissues. The importance of
the proposed method is confirmed by the ablation study
reporting results on the C1 and C2 datasets. It shows that our
augmentation and blending pipeline significantly improves
the detection performance compared to the baseline, which
uses the annotated artifacts directly.
Future work will include further optimization of our meth-
ods for blending the artifacts into images. We will replace the
current graph-based segmentation algorithm with a learning-
based one, like in [14]. Changing the YOLOv5 model to
an architecture with a known better performance on small
objects could further improve the results. Moreover, we will
extend the artifacts library and use artifacts from additional
histology datasets.
V. CONCLUSIONS
We propose a novel method to improve QC by augmenting
and blending real WSI artifacts into other datasets. We
evaluate the proposed approach using a modified YOLOv5
method with additional objective function weighting. We
show an important improvement compared to the baseline.
